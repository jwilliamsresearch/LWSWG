    Understanding how humans conceptualize and interact with places in walking systems

    Introduction to Place Representation

    Place representation in walking systems goes beyond simple geographic coordinates to encompass the rich, multifaceted ways humans understand and interact with spatial environments. Unlike traditional GIS approaches that focus on geometric space, place-based or "platial" approaches emphasize the experiential, cultural, and social dimensions of location.

    For walking applications, this means representing not just where routes go, but how places feel, what they mean to different user communities, and how spatial experiences contribute to the overall walking experience.

    Theoretical Foundations

    The Distinction Between Space and Place

    A fundamental theoretical basis for advanced walking systems is the distinction between space and place. In this context, space is understood as an abstract, geometric, and coordinate-based representation of a location, concerned primarily with quantitative measurements. Place, in contrast, refers to a meaningful location that is imbued with human experience, memory, and social significance. Understanding this distinction is crucial for designing systems that resonate with users on a human level. This involves appreciating concepts from spatial cognition, which studies how humans mentally represent and navigate spatial environments, and place attachment, which describes the emotional and functional bonds that form between people and places.

    Perspectives from Humanistic Geography

    Insights from humanistic geography provide a rich framework for designing place-based systems. Phenomenological approaches, for instance, prioritise the lived experience of place, encouraging a focus on how environments are perceived and engaged with during the act of walking. This leads to a deeper appreciation for the 'sense of place', which encompasses the individual and collective meanings that become attached to specific locations. Related concepts such as 'topophilia', or the love of place, help to explain how affective ties influence spatial behaviour and route choice, while the notion of 'genius loci'—the distinctive atmosphere or character of a place—guides the representation of qualitative, experiential attributes.

    Cognitive Science and Spatial Understanding

    Cognitive science offers valuable insights into the mental models people use for navigation. Research highlights the importance of landmark-based navigation, where distinctive features in the environment serve as crucial spatial anchors for orientation and wayfinding. This contrasts with route knowledge, which is a sequential, turn-by-turn understanding of paths and their connections. At a higher level of abstraction is survey knowledge, which constitutes a map-like mental representation of a spatial layout, allowing for shortcuts and flexible navigation. Furthermore, it is important to consider different spatial reference frames, such as the ego-centric (self-centred) versus the allo-centric (world-centred), which influence how navigational information is processed and communicated.

    Representation Approaches

    Ontological Frameworks

    To formally represent the complex nature of place, structured ontological frameworks are employed. Place ontologies provide formal, explicit representations of place concepts and the relationships between them, moving beyond simple geographic features. These are often supported by spatial ontologies, which offer a hierarchical classification of spatial entities and their topological relations. To capture the dynamic nature of places, activity ontologies can be used to represent the various activities that typically occur in specific locations. Furthermore, cultural ontologies are essential for representing community-specific ways of understanding and categorising place, acknowledging that meaning can be highly context-dependent.

    Semantic Spatial Representation

    Semantic spatial representation involves approaches that encode qualitative meaning alongside quantitative geometric information. A primary method is semantic annotation, which involves attaching descriptive, meaning-based metadata to spatial features. An affordance-based representation takes this further by encoding what actions or behaviours a place enables or constrains for different individuals. This is closely related to activity-based classification, where places are categorised by the primary activities they support, rather than by their physical form alone. Finally, a multi-modal place description aims to create a richer picture by incorporating visual, auditory, and even tactile qualities of a place into its digital representation.

    User-Generated Place Information

    Leveraging crowd-sourced and community-contributed knowledge provides a powerful, bottom-up approach to understanding place. This can be achieved through folk taxonomies, which are community-based classification systems that reflect how local users, rather than experts, categorise places. Narrative place descriptions, such as stories or personal accounts associated with a location, offer rich, qualitative insights into the experiential aspects of place. On a larger scale, social tagging systems allow users to contribute keywords and categories, creating a dynamic and evolving semantic layer. The aggregation of reviews and ratings also serves as a form of collective assessment, providing valuable data on the perceived qualities of a place.

    Key Concepts

    • Platial Information: Data that emphasizes place over space
    • Sense of Place: Subjective meaning attributed to locations
    • Spatial Ontology: Formal knowledge representation for space and place
    • Place Attachment: Emotional bonds with locations

    Representation Types

    • Geometric: Coordinates, shapes, distances
    • Topological: Connectivity, adjacency, containment
    • Semantic: Meaning, categories, functions
    • Experiential: Feelings, memories, associations
    • Social: Community meanings, cultural significance
    • Temporal: Historical changes, seasonal variations

    User Perspectives

    Place representation must account for different user viewpoints:

    • Local residents vs. visitors
    • Different cultural backgrounds
    • Age and mobility differences
    • Activity-specific interests
    • Temporal usage patterns
